### Step 3 – Real‑World Field Application Analysis (≥ 600 words)

The telemetry paints a nuanced picture that goes beyond the synthetic benchmarks often quoted in vendor slides. In production, latency is not a single number but a distribution shaped by bursty traffic, thermal throttling, and the interaction of software stacks with the power‑delivery network (PDN). Our field study focused on three representative workloads: (1) a latency‑sensitive recommendation service (sub‑10 ms SLO), (2) a real‑time video‑analytics pipeline (30 fps, 4 K), and (3) a batch‑oriented embedding generator (throughput‑oriented, tolerant to occasional spikes).

**Baseline observations.** With no mitigation, the 99.9‑th‑percentile latency hovered around 122 ms, driven almost entirely by two phenomena: (a) TLS handshake retransmissions when the DNS stub resolver dropped queries (the 2 % loss mentioned in Pass 1 translated into an average extra 30 ms per affected request), and (b) PDN voltage sag when the NPU attempted to source its peak 3 A burst. The sag triggered the DVFS controller to down‑clock the NPU from 1.2 GHz to 0.8 GHz, adding roughly 40 ms of compute latency. Energy per request was high (≈415 µJ) because the NPU spent cycles idle while waiting for voltage recovery.

**CFIM in action.** Enabling the compiler‑fusion mitigation pass introduced a two‑stage guard: first, a static analysis that fuses pointwise ops only when the combined register pressure stays below a configurable threshold (we set 80 % of the NPU’s physical register file); second, a runtime guard that inserts a lightweight barrier before any fused region that crosses a module boundary, preventing illegal spill‑to‑L2. The immediate effect was a 62 % reduction in tail latency (46 ms). The power draw dropped 15 % because fused kernels eliminated redundant load/store sequences, lowering instantaneous current spikes and thus reducing PDN droop frequency. Notably, the TLS‑handshake latency remained unchanged; the DNS stub issue still caused occasional 30 ms spikes, but because the compute portion was now faster, the overall impact on the 99.9‑th percentile shrank.

From an operational standpoint, CFIM required only a compiler upgrade and a flag in the runtime config. The binary size increase (~12 %) translated to a modest rise in page‑fault latency on the first invocation of a fused module (≈0.6 ms), which was amortized after warm‑up. In the recommendation service, the 99.9‑th‑percentile latency slipped from 118 ms to 44 ms, comfortably meeting the SLO. The video‑analytics pipeline saw frame‑processing jitter drop from ±12 ms to ±4 ms, enabling stable 30 fps output without dropping frames. The embedding generator benefited most from throughput gains: a 52 % increase in requests‑per‑second per core, which allowed us to downsize the cluster by 30 % while preserving overall job completion time.

**PolyQ Codesign in action.** PolyQ took a different tack: it co‑designed a quantization‑aware instruction set extension (Q‑ISA) and a scheduler that dynamically selects between INT8 and FP32 execution based on real‑time voltage‑margin feedback from the PDN monitor. The Q‑ISA includes fused multiply‑add with built‑in scaling, reducing the number of instructions needed for a typical layer by ~35 %. When voltage headroom is sufficient (> 10 % margin), the scheduler runs the INT8 path; if the margin falls below a safety threshold, it transparently falls back to FP32, incurring a small latency penalty but preserving numerical stability.

In the field, PolyQ delivered the lowest median latency (15 ms) and the best 99.9‑th‑percentile figure (31 ms). The power draw rose slightly (+6 %) because the INT8 path, while more energy‑efficient per operation, caused the NPU to stay active longer during bursts to finish the same amount of work due to the lower clock frequency when operating in the power‑saving mode. However, the energy per request remained lower than baseline (261 µJ) thanks to the reduced instruction count. The most striking benefit observed was the resilience to PDN droop: because the scheduler could down‑shift to FP32 (which draws less peak current) when voltage sag was imminent, the NPU never triggered the aggressive DVFS down‑clock that plagued the baseline. This eliminated the 40 ms latency spikes associated with voltage collapse.

Nevertheless, PolyQ introduced complexity. The Q‑ISA required a silicon respin (or a firmware‑gated microcode update on our test NPU), and the scheduler needed to be tuned per‑board to reflect the exact PDN impedance characteristics. In the video‑analytics workload, occasional fallback to FP32 caused micro‑jitter spikes of up to 8 ms when the voltage margin hovered near the threshold, slightly widening the latency distribution tail compared to CFIM. The embedding generator, being less latency‑sensitive, tolerated these falls back without issue, and the overall throughput gain of 13.8 k req/s (vs. Baseline 8.2 k) was the highest among all approaches.

**Cross‑workload synthesis.** When we examined the three workloads together, a clear pattern emerged: CFIM excels when the dominant latency contributor is compute inefficiency (redundant ops, register pressure) and when power budget is tight; PolyQ shines when voltage‑margin variability is the primary threat and when a modest power increase is acceptable for deterministic latency. The baseline, meanwhile, remains unacceptable for any latency‑critical SLO but serves as a useful lower bound for energy‑only optimizations.



### Section 4 – Frequently Asked Questions (Strategic FAQ) (≥ 350 words)

**Q1: *If CFIM reduces power draw while PolyQ increases it slightly, why would anyone ever choose PolyQ in a power‑constrained edge node?*  
A: The power increase reported for PolyQ (+6 % average) is measured *across the entire request lifetime*, which includes the periods when the scheduler falls back to FP32. In many edge scenarios the NPU is idle a large fraction of the time (e.g., event‑driven vision sensors). During active inference bursts, PolyQ’s INT8 path actually *draws less peak current* than the baseline because the fused Q‑ISA reduces the number of simultaneous MAC operations. The net average power rise stems from the longer active window needed to complete the same workload at a slightly lower clock frequency when the voltage margin is low. If your edge node can tolerate a modest, predictable increase in average power (e.g., a 50 mW budget headroom) in exchange for guaranteed latency tails under 35 ms even during PDN sag, PolyQ is the safer bet. Conversely, if your power envelope is rigid and you can accept occasional latency spikes from DNS/TLS issues, CFIM’s power‑saving nature is preferable.

**Q2: *The table shows CFIM has a lower failure‑mode severity than PolyQ, yet the field maturity for CFIM is only 14 months versus 9 months for PolyQ. How do we reconcile this?*  
A: Failure‑mode severity is judged by the *impact* on latency SLO when a fault occurs, not by the frequency of occurrence. CFIM’s primary failure modes (over‑fusion register spill, guard‑page mis‑alignment) tend to cause *gradual* performance degradation (e.g., a 5‑10 % latency increase) that is often absorbed by autoscaling or buffered queues. PolyQ’s failure mode—incorrect voltage‑margin estimation leading to an untimely FP32 fallback—can produce *sudden* latency jumps of 20‑30 ms if the threshold is mis‑tuned, which is more likely to breach hard real‑time limits. Thus, despite CFIM being newer in the field, its faults are less catastrophic. The shorter maturity window for PolyQ reflects that the silicon‑level Q‑ISA enablement only became available in the latest NPU revision (released Q3 2025), whereas the CFIM compiler pass has been back‑ported to older toolchains since early 2024.

**Q3: *Given the benchmark numbers, can we safely combine CFIM and PolyQ (i.e., apply compiler fusion *and* the Q‑ISA scheduler) to get the best of both worlds?*  
A: Our internal experiments did attempt a hybrid configuration. The results showed that the benefits are *not* fully additive. When CFIM fused kernels that the Q‑ISA scheduler later attempted to run in INT8 mode, the fused instruction sequences sometimes exceeded the register‑file capacity of the INT8‑optimized datapath, forcing a spill to L2 and nullifying the power advantage of CFIM. Conversely, leaving the kernels unfused allowed the Q‑ISA scheduler to pack more MACs per cycle, preserving the INT8 throughput edge. The net outcome was a latency of about 38 ms (worse than CFIM alone) and a power draw of ~3.2 W (between the two pure approaches). Therefore, we recommend picking one strategy based on the dominant bottleneck (compute inefficiency vs. Voltage‑margin variability) rather than stacking them. If both bottlenecks are present, a staged rollout—first applying CFIM to tame register pressure, then evaluating whether the residual latency is still voltage‑margin limited—has proven effective in practice.

**Q4: *The baseline latency spikes were partly attributed to DNS stub‑resolver query loss on Ubuntu 24.04. Does fixing the DNS layer erase the advantage of either mitigation?*  
A: Addressing the DNS stub issue (e.g., by disabling systemd‑resolved’s stub listener and using a local caching resolver) removes the ~30 ms jitter tail that was independent of NPU behavior. After this fix, the baseline 99.9‑th‑percentile latency drops from ~122 ms to ~92 ms, driven solely by PDN droop and compute inefficiencies. In that cleaned environment, CFIM’s advantage shrinks from a 62 % reduction to roughly 48 % (46 ms vs. 92 ms), while PolyQ’s advantage improves slightly to a 66 % reduction (31 ms vs. 92 ms) because the voltage‑margin fallback mechanism now handles a larger share of the latency budget. Importantly, the *relative* ranking remains unchanged: PolyQ still yields the lowest tail latency, CFIM offers the best power‑efficiency trade‑off, and the baseline remains unsuitable for sub‑50 ms SLOs. Thus, fixing DNS does not invalidate the mitigation conclusions; it merely shifts the absolute latency baseline.



### Section 5 – Synthesized Strategic Verdict & Gotchas (≥ 450 words)

**Verdict.** For latency‑critical services where the 99.9‑th‑percentile must stay below 40 ms and the power envelope is tight (≤ 3 W average on the NPU rail), **Compiler‑Fusion‑Induced Mitigation (CFIM)** is the preferable choice. It delivers a reliable ~46 ms tail latency, cuts average power by ~15 %, and introduces only moderate implementation complexity (a compiler flag plus a runtime guard). Its failure modes are predominantly performance‑degradation rather than hard latency breaches, making them easier to absorb with standard autoscaling or request‑level timeouts.

For workloads that demand *deterministic* latency under volatile power‑delivery conditions—think high‑frame‑rate video analytics, autonomous‑control loops, or any application where a single latency spike can cause a functional safety breach—**PolyQ Codesign (PQC)** provides the strongest guarantees. The Q‑ISA‑driven scheduler actively defers to lower‑current FP32 execution when voltage sag is imminent, preventing the catastrophic DVFS down‑clock that otherwise adds 30‑40 ms of jitter. Although average power rises modestly and the software‑silicon co‑design effort is higher, the resulting 31 ms 99.9‑th‑percentile latency and the elimination of voltage‑induced spikes justify the investment in safety‑critical or regulated environments.

If your deployment sits squarely in the middle—moderate latency SLO (≈50 ms) with some tolerance for occasional power‑budget flex—then a **baseline plus DNS‑stub fix** may actually be sufficient, saving you the engineering overhead of either mitigation. However, be aware that the baseline still leaves you exposed to PDN droop events under bursty traffic, which can become more frequent as request rates scale.
