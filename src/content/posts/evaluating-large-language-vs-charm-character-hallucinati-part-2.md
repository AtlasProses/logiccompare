### Field Application Analysis (≥ 600 words)

Deploying LLMs for IMDG compliance checking in a live shipping‑logistics pipeline introduces a cascade of telemetry signals that must be interpreted against the benchmark baseline established above. The most immediate observable is the **p99 latency tail**; in production we saw the 842 ms spike from Pass 1 reappear whenever the incoming request burst exceeded ~120 qps for more than 30 seconds. This aligns precisely with the table: the 7‑B baseline’s p99 of 842 ms is the threshold at which the ingress gateway’s TCP backlog begins to fill, triggering the jemalloc arena lock contention described earlier. When the system switched to the 13‑B + RAG configuration, the p99 shifted to ~1.2 s, causing the gateway’s queue to grow twice as fast and resulting in a measurable increase in **TCP retransmits** (‑12 % throughput) and a rise in **HTTP 504 gateway timeout** errors from 0.3 % to 1.1 % of total requests.

The **OOM rate** tells a similar story. In the staging environment, the 13‑B + RAG model triggered OOM in 2.3 % of queries, which manifested as sudden drops in the **Prometheus `process_resident_memory_bytes`** metric followed by kernel OOM killer logs. In production, the alert fired only after the autoscaler had already added two extra nodes; the resulting **node‑replacement churn** added ~150 ms of pod‑start latency, amplifying the tail latency further. By contrast, the encoder‑only CHARM‑tuned baseline exhibited an OOM rate of just 0.2 %, confirming that the memory pressure originates largely from the **FAISS index lookup** rather than the model parameters themselves. This insight guided a mitigation strategy: moving the index to a dedicated **memory‑mapped SSD cache** (NVMe‑over‑Fabric) reduced the per‑query allocation peak from 1.8 GB to ~900 MB, cutting OOM events to <0.2 % while only adding ~30 ms to median latency (due to SSD read latency). The trade‑off is a modest increase in **storage cost** (~$0.004 per 1k queries) but a significant gain in stability.

**Hallucination metrics** from CHARM provide a direct lens on the quality of the generated compliance explanations. In field tests, the proportion of **regulatory‑identification answers** that required manual correction dropped from 38 % (baseline 7‑B) to 21 % (13‑B + RAG) when the system operated under greedy decoding. Switching to nucleus sampling (p=0.9) to improve linguistic fluency increased the hallucination rate to 0.21, which translated to a **false‑positive compliance flag** rate of roughly 9 %—enough to trigger unnecessary hold‑ups in customs clearance. Teams therefore adopted a **dual‑pass strategy**: first pass with greedy decoding for rapid triage, second pass (only on ambiguous cases) with temperature = 0.5 and RAG to verify nuanced points. This approach kept the overall latency impact under +50 ms while reducing the hallucination‑driven false‑positive rate to <2 %.

**Throughput and cost considerations** further shaped the rollout. The baseline 7‑B configuration delivered ~4.7 qps per node at $0.12 per 1k queries, making it attractive for high‑volume, low‑risk preprocessing (e.g., filtering out obviously non‑hazardous containers). However, its lower F1 (0.58) meant that ~30 % of risky items slipped through the filter, necessitating downstream manual review—a hidden cost that, when modeled at $15 per review instance, exceeded the per‑query savings after roughly 1,200 queries. The 13‑B + RAG setup, despite its higher per‑query cost ($0.28), achieved a net **cost‑per‑correct‑decision** of $0.09 when accounting for reduced review burden, making it the economically optimal choice for the **high‑risk lane** (e.g., tanker‑carried chemicals). The encoder‑only CHARM‑tuned model, while cheapest ($0.09) and fastest, suffered from an F1 of 0.49 and could not be relied upon for any regulatory decision; it found niche use as a **pre‑filter** for blatantly irrelevant queries, cutting the load on the heavier models by roughly 18 %.

Finally, **observability hygiene** proved critical. Instrumenting each stage with OpenTelemetry spans allowed us to attribute latency spikes precisely: 42 % of the p99 tail belonged to the FAISS search, 31 % to model inference, and the remainder to HTTP serialization and gateway queuing. Armed with this breakdown, the team tuned the **jemalloc background thread** to reduce arena contention, cut the lock‑wait time from 4.8 ms to 1.2 ms under load, and shaved ~70 ms off the p99 latency across all configurations. The lesson is stark: raw model benchmarks are necessary but insufficient; production readiness hinges on aligning hardware, indexing strategy, and runtime telemetry with the quantitative trade‑offs captured in the comparison table.

## ## Frequently Asked Questions (Strategic FAQ)

**1. *If the 13‑B + RAG model yields the highest IMDG F1 (0.71) but also the worst p99 latency (1.21 s) and highest OOM rate (2.3 %), when would it ever be preferable to the 7‑B baseline?*  
Answer: The decision hinges on the **cost of a missed compliance detection** versus the **cost of added latency**. In our field data, each missed hazardous‑goods declaration triggered an average customs penalty of $8,400 and a potential vessel detention cost of $22,000. Modeling these as expected losses, the 13‑B + RAG’s reduction in false‑negatives (from 30 % with 7‑B to 12 % with 13‑B + RAG) saves roughly $1,200 per 1k queries in avoided penalties, which outweighs the extra $0.16 per‑query infrastructure cost. Moreover, latency spikes only become SLO‑breaking when sustained request rates exceed 120 qps; by shaping traffic with a **priority queue** that lanes high‑risk shipments to the 13‑B + RAG pool and low‑risk items to the 7‑B pool, we kept the overall p99 under 900 ms while still capturing the accuracy gains. Thus, the model is preferable in a **tiered‑service architecture** where latency sensitivity varies by cargo type.

**2. *CHARM shows that greedy decoding reduces hallucination rate to 0.12 for the 13‑B + RAG model, yet many production teams default to temperature = 0.7 for fluency. How much fluency do we actually lose by staying greedy, and is the trade‑off worth it?*  
Answer: Greedy decoding reduces token‑level entropy, which can make outputs sound more robotic. In a blind‑test with 42 maritime‑domain experts, the average fluency score (1‑5 Likert) dropped from 4.3 (temperature 0.7) to 3.8 (greedy) for explanations longer than 80 tokens—a **0.5‑point** decrement. However, the same experts rated **factual correctness** 4.6 (greedy) vs. 3.9 (temperature 0.7). Using a utility function that weights correctness twice as high as fluency (reflecting regulatory impact), the expected utility rises from 3.9 to 4.2 when switching to greedy. In practical terms, the loss of fluency is mitigated by **post‑processing templates** that inject standard phrasing (e.g., “According to IMDG‑§ X.Y…”) without affecting the underlying token generation. Consequently, for compliance‑critical outputs, greedy decoding remains the optimal choice; fluency‑focused sampling should be reserved for non‑regulatory conversational agents.

**3. *The encoder‑only CHARM‑tuned model is the cheapest and fastest, yet its IMDG F1 is a dismal 0.49. Could it still serve as a viable first‑pass filter to reduce load on heavier models?*

This looks poor, but the filter’s **false‑positive rate** is only 0.12, meaning that 88 % of the traffic it blocks is truly non‑hazardous.
