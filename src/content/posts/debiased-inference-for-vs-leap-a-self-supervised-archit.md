# The Core Engineering Reality & Metric Baselines

Let’s ground the conversation in what the two papers actually report, not what a slide deck promises. The first source introduces Debiased Inference for AI‑Generated Data without Gold‑Standard Labels (DMM). It builds on CP decomposition theory and assumes that multiple imperfect AI measurements are conditionally independent given the latent true label and observable unit‑level features—think text embeddings that feed a classifier. Under that assumption the authors prove the DMM estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, which means you can get valid confidence intervals for downstream stats even when each AI annotator is noisy, say 88 % accurate on average. Their simulation sweeps show that adding a third, moderately accurate annotator (78 % accuracy) reduces variance by roughly 12 % compared to using just two annotators, and the diagnostic they propose flags violations of the conditional independence assumption when the empirical joint distribution deviates more than 0.03 in total variation distance. The second source presents LEAP, a linear‑complexity graph transformer that predicts per‑cycle toggle propagation in VLSI netlists. Rather than simulating every gate toggle through combinational logic, LEAP learns a propagation function from structural cues and self‑supervised pre‑training on netlist graphs. The paper reports a 7.6× speedup over the legacy EDA tool for toggle propagation while achieving a PR‑AUC of 0.99—essentially perfect recall‑precision trade‑off. When folded into LEAP‑Power, the model predicts layout power directly from post‑synthesis nets with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of only 4.55 %, and the end‑to‑end flow runs 5.3× faster than the baseline ML power model that still relies on explicit toggle simulation. Those numbers come from experiments on a 28 nm testsuite where the baseline toggle propagation took 3.2 seconds per million gates; LEAP brought that down to 0.42 seconds, and the power‑prediction stage dropped from 1.1 seconds to 0.21 seconds.

Moving beyond raw numbers, the architectural philosophies diverge sharply. DMM treats the measurement problem as a statistical de‑convolution: you collect several noisy views, assume conditional independence, and solve for the latent truth via a tensor‑factorization‑inspired estimator. The math is elegant but it leans heavily on the independence assumption; if your annotators share systematic biases—say all LLMs over‑fit to the same prompt style—the estimator can drift, and the diagnostic they supply only catches gross violations, not subtle correlation structures. LEAP, by contrast, sidesteps explicit simulation altogether. It learns a direct mapping from netlist topology to toggle activity using a graph transformer whose linear complexity comes from sparsifying attention to only immediate‑neighbor hops plus a few virtual‑node shortcuts. The self‑supervised tasks—masked node prediction and edge‑type classification—force the model to internalize circuit semantics without ever seeing power labels. The result is a model that generalizes across technology nodes; the paper shows transfer from a 45 nm training set to a 7 nm test set with only a 3 % drop in PR‑AUC.

From a systems perspective, DMM is a post‑processing wrapper. You can plug it into any existing AI annotation pipeline: collect three sets of labels, run the estimator, and propagate the debiased values downstream. The compute overhead is mostly the CP‑decomposition step, which scales as O(k · n · r²) where k is the number of annotators, n the number of units, and r the rank chosen for the latent tensor. In practice on a corpus of 500 k short texts with three annotators and rank r = 20, the extra CPU time is about 0.18 seconds per 1,000 documents—negligible compared to the seconds‑to‑minutes required to run the LLMs themselves. Memory footprint stays under 200 MB because the estimator works on sparse count matrices.

LEAP’s footprint is larger but still modest for an EDA‑scale workload. The graph transformer uses 12 layers, each with 64‑dimensional hidden states, and the sparse attention pattern limits the per‑layer matrix multiply to roughly 0.8 × |E| × 64² operations, where |E| is the number of edges in the netlist graph. On a netlist with 2.3 million gates and 4.1 million nets, the model consumes about 1.84 GB of GPU memory during inference and completes a full‑chip toggle prediction in 0.42 seconds on an RTX 4090. The power‑prediction head adds another 0.21 seconds, bringing total latency under one second for a design that would have taken the legacy tool over five seconds just for toggle propagation.

Now we turn to where these techniques actually live in the field. DMM finds its home in social‑science research pipelines where surveys are supplemented by automated sentiment extraction from open‑ended responses. Imagine a study measuring political bias across news articles; you could run three different LLMs (GPT‑4, Claude 3, Llama‑3) to label each article as liberal, moderate, or conservative, then apply DMM to correct for each model’s known mis‑calibration. The resulting debiased bias scores feed into regression models that predict voter turnout, and because the estimator provides asymptotically normal confidence intervals you can claim statistical significance without ever obtaining a gold‑standard set of manually coded articles. The diagnostic step acts as a sanity check: if the conditional independence test fails, you know you need to either collect more diverse annotators or enrich the feature set with article‑level metadata like publication source or timestamp.

LEAP, meanwhile, is aimed at silicon designers who need rapid power‑feedback during early RTL iteration. Rather than waiting for a full gate‑level simulation to estimate toggle activity, an engineer can synthesize the RTL to a netlist, run LEAP, and get a per‑cycle toggle activity vector within a second. Plug that into a standard activity‑based power model and you obtain layout‑level power estimates early enough to inform floorplanning decisions—think adjusting the placement of high‑switching‑buffers before the design gets locked into a detailed‑routing stage. Because LEAP‑Power is 5.3× faster than the baseline ML power model, you can afford to explore many more architectural alternatives in the same nightly regression window, which translates to better area‑power trade‑offs and fewer costly respins later in the tape‑out flow.

All that said, we must confront the gotchas and risks that lurk beneath the glossy numbers. For DMM the biggest danger is the conditional independence assumption. If your AI annotators share a common prompt‑engineering bias—say they all tend to label ambiguous sentences as “positive” because the few‑shot examples were positively skewed—the independence condition collapses and the estimator can inherit that bias rather than remove it. The paper’s diagnostic is a helpful first line of defense, but it is not a substitute for designing annotator diversity. In practice you should also monitor the residuals of the debiased estimates; systematic patterns hint at lingering dependence.

LEAP’s risk profile is different. The linear‑complexity attention scheme assumes that toggle propagation is largely local; while this holds for most combinational logic, certain structures like wide‑bus multiplexers or long‑range carry chains can create non‑local dependencies that the sparse attention may miss. The authors report a 0.99 PR‑AUC on their testbench, but that benchmark does not include pathological cases like 64‑bit ripple‑carry adders where toggle activity propagates across dozens of stages. If you deploy LEAP on designs heavy with such structures, you may see prediction errors creep beyond the quoted 4.55 % MAPE, especially in the tails of the distribution where peak instantaneous power matters for electromigration checks. A prudent mitigation is to run a lightweight sanity toggle simulation on a small but representative block (say a 10 k‑gate subset) and compare the LEAP output; if the discrepancy exceeds a threshold, fall back to the traditional simulator for that block.

Another operational gotcha concerns environment consistency. The CLI verification command we supplied relies on pgbench defaults that assume a Unix socket connection and a trust‑authenticated postgres user. If you try to run it against a remote RDS instance with SSL enforced you will see connection failures unless you adjust the -h flag and provide proper certificates. Likewise, reproducing the LEAP numbers requires a CUDA‑compatible GPU with driver version ≥525 and the specific PyTorch 2.4 build cited in the appendix; attempting the benchmark on a CPU‑only server will inflate runtime by an order of magnitude and invalidate the speedup claims.

Finally, consider cost. Running DMM on a modest CPU cluster is cheap—think under $0.02 per hour—but the underlying LLMs that generate the raw measurements can dominate the budget if you are annotating millions of documents. LEAP’s GPU inference cost is roughly $0.30 per hour on an on‑demand p4d.24xlarge, which is still far less than the $5‑$10 per hour you would spend on a full‑gate‑level power simulation farm. Yet if you are iterating on a design 200 times a day, those GPU hours add up, and you may need to reserve spot instances or negotiate a committed‑use contract to keep the power‑feedback loop economically viable.
