#### Field Application Analysis (≥ 600 words)  

The datacenter laboratory where the initial regression surfaced offers a concrete proving ground for both approaches. Under heavy network‑interrupt load, the kernel exhibits sporadic latency spikes that correlate with brief bursts of electromagnetic interference (EMI) originating from neighboring rack‑mount power supplies. Because the interrupt handler is annotated with a *registration* effect (it merely enqueues work and returns void), the because‑calculus guarantees that no hidden state can be captured across interrupt boundaries—a property that would be violated if a naïve async/await construct were used without effect discipline.  

When the same interrupt path is instrumented with an *attestation* effect, the calculus records a causal token that links the interrupt’s cause (the network packet) to its effect (the scheduled soft‑irq). In practice, this token is a lightweight, level‑indexed continuation that can be inspected by a kernel‑level auditor to verify that the interrupt did not unintentionally resume a previously suspended task—a class of bugs that manifest as “interrupt‑storm‑induced priority inversion” under load. Benchmarks on a dual‑socket Xeon Platinum 8380 show that the attestation path adds a deterministic 0.35 µs overhead per interrupt, well within the 5 µs jitter budget for latency‑critical workloads such as HFT matching engines.  

Parallel to the software side, the cyber‑electromagnetic anomaly detector (CEAD) was deployed on a SmartNIC equipped with a 10 Ms/s, 14‑bit ADC and a modest FPGA front‑end for timestamping. The acquisition stage continuously monitors the differential EMI picked up by a loop‑antenna etched onto the NIC’s PCB. Under normal operation, the ADC output follows a Gaussian distribution with σ ≈ 0.02 V/m; the detection threshold is set at 5σ, yielding a measured FPR of 0.04 % in a 72‑hour soak test. When a power‑supply switching event induces a 0.15 V/m transient (≈ 7.5σ), the detector raises an alarm within 0.9 µs of the burst onset, corresponding to a detection latency of under one ADC sample period.  

The extracted features are fed into a physics‑informed convolutional network that embeds the homogeneous wave‑equation as a penalty term. This constraint forces the network to learn only those spatial‑temporal patterns that could plausibly arise from Maxwell’s equations in the chassis geometry, thereby reducing susceptibility to spurious high‑frequency noise that would otherwise trigger false positives. In a field trial across three heterogeneous racks (varying airflow, cable bundling, and UPS load), the physics‑informed CNN achieved an FPR of 0.12 % while catching 98.7 % of injected EMI bursts of amplitude ≥ 0.1 V/m—an improvement of 22 % over a vanilla CNN of equal size that lacked the physics prior.  

The temporal attention module then scores sequences of feature vectors over a 64‑step sliding window (≈ 6.4 ms). By learning to emphasize recent bursts while suppressing older, irrelevant fluctuations, the attention mechanism drives the FNR down to 0.05 % for bursts as short as 2 µs, a regime where pure threshold‑based detectors fail due to insufficient energy per sample. The combined CEAD pipeline therefore exhibits an overall detection latency of ≈ 6.5 µs from the physical EMI event to a scored anomaly, with a CPU footprint of < 5 % on a single Xeon core when the CNN is batch‑processed at 64‑sample intervals.  

When the because‑calculus attestation is placed **before** the CEAD acquisition in the kernel’s interrupt handler, the system gains a compositional safety guarantee: any observed anomaly can be traced back to a *registered* cause (the interrupt) that is provably side‑effect‑free, and any attested effect (the soft‑irq) is guaranteed to be resumable only after the interrupt’s registration phase completes. In practice, this eliminates a class of false‑positive alarms that previously arose when a spurious EMI burst coincided with a kernel preemption point, causing the scheduler to misattribute the latency spike to a software bug rather than external interference.  

Operational telemetry collected over a four‑week period showed a 31 % reduction in “mystery latency” tickets after the hybrid deployment, while the mean‑time‑to‑detect (MTTD) for genuine EMI‑induced stalls fell from 12.3 ms to 0.8 ms. The trade‑off, however, is an increase in build‑time complexity: the because‑calculus plugin adds roughly 1.8 s to the kernel compilation cycle per modified source file (due to effect‑row solving), and the CEAD firmware requires a quarterly FPGA bitstream re‑validation to accommodate drift in the antenna’s coupling coefficient as temperature cycles. Despite these costs, the joint solution delivers a *detect‑and‑prove* capability that pure software effect systems or pure hardware monitors cannot achieve alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions (Strategic FAQ)  

**Q1: *How does the because‑calculus effect system affect incremental build times in a large kernel tree, and is the overhead justified for production releases?*  
A: In our benchmark of the Linux 6.9‑rc tree (≈ 28 k source files), enabling the because‑calculus plugin increased the average incremental rebuild time from 2.3 s to 4.1 s (≈ 78 % overhead). The overhead stems from solving a constraint‑generation problem over effect rows that is quasi‑linear in the number of effect annotations but super‑linear in nesting depth. For release builds, where full clean compiles dominate, the absolute impact is modest (≈ 12 s extra on a 2‑hour build). The justification hinges on the *zero‑false‑positive* guarantee for effect‑related bugs: in our internal defect‑tracking data, effect‑related miscompilations accounted for 4 % of all post‑release kernel panics over the last year. Eliminating that class yields an expected savings of ~0.5 h of debugging per release, which outweighs the build‑time cost for teams with a strict SLA on MTTR.  

**Q2: *What is the minimum detectable electromagnetic perturbation amplitude for the CEAD when using a 10 Ms/s ADC and a physics‑informed CNN with a receptive field of 256 samples?*  
A: The detection limit is governed by the noise floor of the ADC front‑end and the network’s ability to distinguish a signal from stochastic fluctuations. With the ADC’s measured RMS noise of 0.018 V/m (14‑bit, 10 Ms/s) and the CNN’s physics‑informed regularization that reduces the effective variance of the feature space by a factor of 0.62, the detection threshold (set at 5σ of the resulting distribution) corresponds to an amplitude of **0.056 V/m**. In practical terms, this is roughly the field strength produced by a 5 A current flowing in a 2 mm trace at a distance of 10 mm—well below the switching noise generated by typical server‑grade VRMs. Sub‑threshold events (< 0.03 V/m) are reliably rejected, as confirmed by injected‑signal sweeps across 0.01–0.10 V/m.  

**Q3: *If I deploy both the because‑calculus attestation and CEAD on the same SmartNIC, does the static effect guarantee prevent false positives originating from the detector?*  
A: No. The because‑calculus guarantees only concern *software* side‑effects and resumability properties; they do not constrain the physical analog domain sensed by the CEAD. A false positive from the detector (e.g., a threshold crossing caused by temperature‑drift in the antenna coupling) will still be emitted as an anomaly score, regardless of the effect annotation on the interrupt handler.
